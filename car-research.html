Fuel & Powertrain Guide

Choosing the wrong fuel type for your lifestyle is the most common (and expensive) mistake car buyers make.

Petrol: The UK All-Rounder

Petrol engines ignite the air/fuel mixture using spark plugs. They are smoother, quieter, and generally cheaper to buy and maintain than diesels. Modern petrol engines often use turbocharging and direct injection to provide good fuel economy and power.

Advantages

  • Smoother and quieter than diesel
  • Cheaper to service and maintain
  • No DPF (Diesel Particulate Filter) issues
  • Better suited to short journeys and city driving
  • Immediate power delivery

Common Faults & Considerations

  • Wet Belts: Many modern petrols (Ford 1.0 EcoBoost, Vauxhall 1.2 PureTech) use a timing belt submerged in oil. These degrade and can destroy the engine if not replaced.
  • Carbon Build-Up: Direct Injection (GDI) engines can get carbon build-up on the intake valves, causing rough running.
  • Ignition Coils: Spark plug coil packs are a common failure, causing misfires.

Diesel: Built for High-Mileage Driving

Diesel engines use compression ignition (no spark plugs) to ignite the fuel. They offer incredible torque and high fuel economy on long journeys. However, modern emission controls make them unsuitable for short city trips.

Advantages

  • Excellent fuel economy on motorways
  • High torque (great for towing)
  • Engines often last 200,000+ miles if maintained
  • Strong low-end power

Common Faults & Considerations

  • DPF (Diesel Particulate Filter): The #1 issue. Short trips block the filter, preventing regeneration. Replacing one costs £1,000 - £2,500+.
  • EGR Valve: Exhaust Gas Recirculation valves get coked up with soot, causing rough idling.
  • Dual Mass Flywheel (DMF): Diesels wear out DMFs, causing violent vibrations. A clutch + DMF replacement is £900 - £1,800+.

Mild Hybrid (MHEV)

A mild hybrid uses a small battery (often 48V) and a starter-generator to assist the combustion engine. It cannot drive the car on electric power alone, but it helps with fuel economy and smooths out the engine.

Advantages

  • Cheaper than a full hybrid or PHEV
  • Slightly better fuel economy and smoother stop/start
  • No charging cables required
  • Often cheaper to tax than equivalent petrol

Common Faults & Considerations

  • Starter-Generator: If the 48V unit fails, the car may not start.
  • Battery: The 48V battery can degrade over time.
  • Complexity: Adds electrical components to traditional petrol engines.

Full Hybrid (HEV)

Combines a combustion engine with an electric motor and battery. The car can drive on electric power alone for short distances (like in a car park). The engine charges the battery; you don't plug it in.

Advantages

  • Incredible reliability (especially Toyota/Lexus)
  • Superb fuel economy in towns and cities
  • Automatic gearbox as standard
  • No range anxiety or charging cables

Common Faults & Considerations

  • Battery Cooling: Ensure the hybrid battery cooling fan is clean and not blocked by dust/pet hair.
  • Battery Degradation: Older hybrids may lose some efficiency, though usually very slowly.
  • Not as efficient on the motorway as a diesel.

Plug-in Hybrid (PHEV)

A PHEV has a larger battery that you charge from the mains. It can usually drive 20-50 miles on electric power alone before switching to petrol. If you don't charge it, you are just carrying heavy batteries around.

Advantages

  • Electric driving for short commutes
  • No range anxiety (petrol engine backup)
  • Low Benefit-in-Kind (BiK) tax for company car drivers

Common Faults & Considerations

  • Charging Faults: The onboard charger or charging port can fail.
  • Heavy: The extra weight means higher tyre and brake wear.
  • Real-world economy: Terrible if you don't plug them in regularly.

Electric (EV)

Fully electric cars use a high-voltage battery, an inverter, and an electric motor. They offer silent, instant acceleration and incredibly low running costs.

Advantages

  • Lowest running costs (if charging at home)
  • Zero road tax and no ULEZ charges
  • Almost zero maintenance (no oil, no belts, no exhaust)
  • Instant torque

Common Faults & Considerations

  • 12V Battery: Just like petrol cars, EVs have a 12V battery that can fail, causing the car to refuse to unlock or start.
  • Battery Degradation: Older EVs lose range. Check the State of Health (SoH).
  • Charging System: Charging ports can get damaged; public chargers can be unreliable.
